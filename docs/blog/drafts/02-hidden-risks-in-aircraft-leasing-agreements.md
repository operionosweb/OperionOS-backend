# 10 Hidden Risks in Aircraft Leasing Agreements That Aviation Businesses Should Understand

Aircraft leases are negotiated documents, not interchangeable forms. A provision that matters on one transaction may not apply to another aircraft, operator, jurisdiction or commercial structure. The ten areas below are a **review prompt**, not a claim that every lease contains the same terms or a substitute for transaction-specific legal, technical and financial advice.

For each topic, read the executed lease together with amendments, side letters, maintenance-program documents, delivery records and any incorporated schedules. Confirm which version is operative before treating a summary or spreadsheet as authoritative.

## 1. Operational control and regulatory responsibility

The parties’ commercial labels do not, on their own, answer who has operational control or which regulatory obligations apply. The FAA’s AC 91-37B explains operational-control issues for U.S.-registered aircraft and notes the applicability of Truth-in-Leasing requirements to specified large aircraft. The regulation itself includes exceptions and conditions.

**Review:** aircraft registration, operating certificate, lease type, responsible operator, regulatory allocation, required notices and document copies. Ask aviation counsel or the competent authority to confirm which rules apply; do not generalize U.S. requirements to other jurisdictions.

## 2. Rent, escalation and payment mechanics

The headline rent is only one part of the payment obligation. Check currency, due date, business-day convention, late interest, taxes, deposits, escalation triggers, index source, observation date, calculation method, caps or floors and correction process. A change to a reference value does not necessarily change rent unless the contract says how and when it does.

**Review:** clause, schedule, invoice fields, approved reference source and how a disputed calculation is raised.

## 3. Maintenance reserves and reimbursement eligibility

Maintenance-reserve clauses can define payments by event, flight hour, cycle or calendar period. They may also specify when a lessee can claim reimbursement, what evidence is required, how amounts are calculated and whether unused balances are retained. The industry treatment varies by negotiated lease.

**Review:** covered components, rate basis, reporting units, eligible work, claim windows, supporting records, offsets, caps and end-of-term treatment. A reserve payment should not be assumed to be refundable cash.

## 4. Return conditions and compensation

Return conditions may describe the aircraft and its components at lease end, while a shortfall may trigger maintenance work or a compensation calculation. Small differences in definitions, measurement dates, records or permitted alternatives can have material operational consequences.

**Review:** required condition by component, measurement method, maintenance-status evidence, delivery timing, cure options, compensation formula and who approves the return package. The [National Law Review’s discussion of engine maintenance reserves and return compensation](https://natlawreview.com/article/maximizing-returns-through-effective-management-engine-performance-restoration-five) is useful context, but it is commentary on examples—not a universal lease rule.

## 5. Records, technical data and evidence handover

An aircraft may be physically serviceable while a record, certificate or traceability item is missing or incomplete. The lease and applicable rules determine what has to be maintained, delivered and accepted. For covered U.S. leases, 14 CFR § 91.23 addresses specified maintenance and inspection information in a Truth-in-Leasing clause.

**Review:** required records, format, language, recipient, inspection rights, cure period, retention and the evidence needed to close each obligation.

## 6. Maintenance scope, approvals and access

The agreement may allocate routine and non-routine maintenance, modifications, approved programs, shop selection, access, records and lessor consent. A mismatch between lease text, the approved maintenance program and operational practice can emerge only when work is scheduled.

**Review:** responsibilities, approval lead times, technical standards, parts and repair restrictions, audit/access rights, notice, cost allocation and escalation for unscheduled work. Have the technical organization validate engineering details.

## 7. Utilisation limits and reporting

Hours, cycles, permitted use, geographic restrictions or reporting intervals can affect rent, reserves, maintenance planning or return status where the lease expressly links them. Units and reporting cut-offs matter: “flight hour” or “cycle” may have a contract-specific definition.

**Review:** definitions, data source, reporting cadence, correction rights, excess-use rate, exception process and reconciliation against operational records.

## 8. Insurance, indemnities and liability boundaries

Insurance schedules, additional-insured requirements, deductibles, waiver language, notice duties and indemnity provisions interact, but they are not the same thing. A certificate of insurance alone may not answer every coverage or liability question.

**Review:** insured parties, policy limits and period, required endorsements, insurer rating criteria if stated, claims notice, exclusions, deductibles, subrogation language and the allocation of loss. Ask a qualified aviation insurance adviser to assess coverage rather than infer it from a summary.

## 9. Defaults, cure periods and termination steps

A missed payment, reporting failure, insurance lapse or other breach may have different notice, cure, remedy and termination mechanics. Cross-defaults or document-specific remedies can be easy to overlook when obligations are tracked separately.

**Review:** event definition, materiality threshold, notice recipient, cure period, evidence of cure, remedy sequence, suspension or termination rights, and the documents cross-referenced. Do not treat an internal alert as a legal notice.

## 10. Amendments, side letters and document precedence

An amendment may change a clause without replacing the entire lease. A side letter, schedule or consent may qualify the main agreement. Comparing only the current-looking PDF can therefore miss the operative term or an obligation that survives.

**Review:** amendment dates, affected sections, precedence language, effective conditions, signatories, consents and whether downstream obligation records have been updated. Preserve the source and reviewer decision for each changed term.

## Make the review operational

For each accepted obligation, record the source clause and version, responsible party, trigger or due date, required evidence, calculation rule (if any), reviewer, next action and closure record. Keep “not found,” “not verified” and “not applicable” distinct. Then coordinate with legal, engineering, finance and operations on the genuinely high-impact items.
